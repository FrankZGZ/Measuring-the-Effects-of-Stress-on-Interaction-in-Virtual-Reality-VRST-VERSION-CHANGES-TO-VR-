\section{Introduction}
% the world
Immersive systems are increasingly used for remote operation~\citep{haidegger2011surgery}, simulation-based training~\citep{radianti2020systematic}, and social interaction~\citep{li2021social,wei2022communication}. In these settings, stress may arise from task demands~\citep{arora2010stress} or be deliberately induced in training~\citep{kleygrewe2024virtual}, social-evaluative scenarios~\citep{helminen2019metaanalysis}, and exposure therapy~\citep{carl2019virtual}. Prior research suggests that stressful immersive experiences can affect users' physiological responses~\citep{vandammen2022evoking}, speech~\citep{dillon2021real}, and task performance~\citep{peterson2018effects}. Understanding how acute stress is reflected in movement, speech, and task timing can inform the evaluation and design of VR interaction.

Immersive tasks often combine visual attention, manual control, speech production, working memory, and planning, meaning a single global performance measure offers limited insight into how interaction changes under stress. Furthermore, theoretical accounts do not predict a uniform direction for these effects. On one hand, acute stress can disrupt prefrontal function~\citep{arnsten2009stress,qin2009acute}, impairing working memory and executive control~\citep{shields2016effects}. On the other hand, acute social-evaluative stress (such as that induced by the Trier Social Stress Test, TSST~\citep{kirschbaum1993trier}) is accompanied by elevated state anxiety and physiological arousal~\citep{allen2014biological}, which can trigger compensatory or selective mechanisms~\citep{eysenck2007anxiety,easterbrook1959effect}. Consequently, observed task performance under acute stress may decline, remain stable, or even improve depending on the task structure. This variability motivates examining both the direction and effect size of change across individual interaction measures in tasks with distinct cognitive and motor demands.

% Several theoretical perspectives suggest that the effects of acute stress do not share a single direction. Research on stress-related disruption to prefrontal function~\citep{arnsten2009stress,qin2009acute} suggests that acute stress may affect working memory and aspects of executive control~\citep{shields2016effects}. Acute stress induced by the Trier Social Stress Test (TSST)~\citep{kirschbaum1993trier} can be accompanied by elevated state anxiety and physiological arousal~\citep{allen2014biological}. And attentional control theory~\citep{eysenck2007anxiety} distinguishes performance effectiveness from processing efficiency, suggesting that additional effort may sustain observed performance under anxiety. Attentional narrowing~\citep{easterbrook1959effect} offers another possibility: heightened emotional arousal may narrow the range of cues used, potentially benefiting performance when task-irrelevant cues are excluded. Studies of acute stress have also reported improved selectivity for task-relevant information~\citep{chajut2003selective} and increased shielding of action goals at the expense of cognitive flexibility~\citep{plessow2011inflexibly}. Together, these accounts suggest that observed performance may decline, remain stable, or improve under particular conditions. They therefore motivate examining the direction and magnitude of change in individual measures across tasks with different demands.

We examine acute stress within the perspective of situationally induced impairments and disabilities (SIID) research~\citep{sears2003computers,sarsenbayeva2019measuring}. Multiple Resource Theory (MRT)~\citep{wickens2008multiple} guides the selection of three controlled probes spanning demands common in immersive interaction: target acquisition for visuomotor control, reading aloud for verbal production, and the Tower of London for executive planning (Figure~\ref{fig:mrt}). The same participants completed these tasks in one VR environment at baseline, after a VR adaptation of the Trier Social Stress Test (VR-TSST)~\citep{liszio2018relaxing}, and after a recovery period. We examine each task through its own measures and baseline, alongside state anxiety and cardiovascular measurements collected across the experimental sequence.
%res
State anxiety rose after the VR-TSST, and mean HR increased and root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) decreased during the Induction window. In the subsequent probes, mean fundamental frequency ($F_0$) during reading aloud increased by approximately 3\%, and Tower of London execution time was shorter after induction, whereas target acquisition and Tower of London planning measures showed no phase differences. Although state anxiety remained elevated at the end of the Post-Stress round, heart rate during that round did not differ from the Baseline round.

% Immersive systems are increasingly used for remote operation~\citep{haidegger2011surgery}, simulation-based training~\citep{radianti2020systematic}, and socially interactive environments~\citep{li2021social,wei2022communication}. In these settings, users rarely engage in a single mode of interaction. Instead, they coordinate manual control, spoken communication, and decision making within the same task. Such coordination draws on multiple, partially independent cognitive resources including processing stages, processing codes, perceptual modalities, and response modalities~\citep{wickens2008multiple} that may compete for limited capacity, and is plausibly vulnerable to disruption when users are under acute stress -- a common situational factor in real-world immersive use. Whether stress degrades VR interaction uniformly, or selectively affects components with different resource demands, remains an open question.

% Prior work on situationally-induced impairments and disabilities (SIID) has typically studied stress and task performance in non-immersive settings and on isolated tasks~\citep{sears2003computers,sarsenbayeva2019measuring}, without explicitly relating stress effects to the resource profiles of the interaction components involved. Without such a framing, it is difficult to anticipate which aspects of immersive interaction are most likely to break down under stress, or to design adaptive support for the specific component being affected, such as manual selection, speech production, or planning and execution.

% To address this, we adopt Multiple Resource Theory (MRT)~\citep{wickens2008multiple} as a task-selection framework. MRT models human performance as depending on multiple partially independent resource channels, providing a principled basis for decomposing complex VR interaction into components with distinct demand profiles (Figure~\ref{fig:mrt}). Guided by MRT, we selected three controlled tasks that recur across immersive systems: a \textit{target acquisition} task~\citep{li2025estimating} for perceptual-motor interaction, a \textit{reading aloud} task~\citep{dillon2021real} for verbal production, and a \textit{Tower of London} task~\citep{shallice1982specific} for executive planning. Rather than directly quantifying resource competition, we use these tasks as an initial empirical step toward understanding how stress modulates behaviour across different resource profiles.

% We instantiate this design in a controlled experiment using the Virtual Reality Trier Social Stress Test (VR-TSST)~\citep{liszio2018relaxing}, combining behavioural performance, speech features, and physiological responses. The results do not support a uniform degradation account. In particular, the three interaction components show distinct patterns and magnitudes of change under stress, indicating that situational impairments in VR are task-specific rather than global.

\textbf{Contributions} (1)~We extend situationally-induced impairments and disabilities (SIID) research to immersive VR by examining acute stress across three recurring interaction tasks: target acquisition, reading aloud task, and Tower of London planning, alongside subjective and physiological stress measures. (2)~We provide a task-specific account of changes following acute stress induction, reporting the direction, magnitude, and uncertainty of movement, vocal, and planning-related measures, with exploratory analyses identified explicitly.  (3)~We draw out implications for stress-aware VR systems, arguing that adaptive support should jointly consider the user's stress state and the resource demands of the current task.

% As immersive systems are increasingly used in scenarios such as remote operation~\citep{haidegger2011surgery}, simulation-based training~\citep{radianti2020systematic}, and socially interactive environments~\citep{li2021social,wei2022communication}, users are often required to coordinate multiple forms of interaction within the same setting, including manual control, spoken communication, and decision making. Such complex interaction does not rely on a single cognitive process, but instead requires the coordination of multiple resource channels that may compete for limited capacity. Under stress, this coordination may be further disrupted, potentially leading to uneven or task-specific changes in performance rather than uniform degradation.
