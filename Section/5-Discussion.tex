
\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Selective Changes after Stress Induction without a General Performance Decline}
\label{sec:discussion-dynamics}

% This study did not aim to propose a deployed stress-adaptive VR system, but to characterize how acute stress alters behavior across different components of interaction.

Across the three task probes, we found no evidence of a general decline in performance after stress induction. Although the VR-TSST successfully induced stress, no statistically significant changes were detected in movement time, throughput, or endpoint offset during target acquisition; reading duration, speech rate, or active speech ratio during reading aloud; or first-move latency and move count in the Tower of London task. Mean fundamental frequency (\(F_0\)) increased modestly, by approximately 3\%, during reading aloud. Tower of London execution time and total duration were also significantly shorter in Post-Stress than in Baseline.


The three perspectives introduced earlier predict different directions of change: performance may decline, remain unchanged, or improve. Acute stress has been shown to impair working memory and cognitive flexibility~\citep{shields2016effects}, including after the TSST~\citep{luethi2009stress,alexander2007beta}, yet no corresponding decline was detected in the present task probes. First-move latency and move count showed no significant changes, whereas execution time was significantly shorter after induction. This pattern is compatible with the more focused or action-oriented response suggested by attentional narrowing accounts~\citep{easterbrook1959effect,plessow2011inflexibly}. The absence of a general performance decline is also consistent with attentional control theory~\citep{eysenck2007anxiety}, which proposes that additional effort or compensatory control can help maintain performance under anxiety. Seven participants reported feeling more focused during the post-stress tasks, suggesting that increased task engagement may have helped sustain performance under elevated anxiety.

These findings also extend the perspective of research on situationally induced impairments and disabilities (SIID) ~\citep{sears2003computers,sarsenbayeva2019situationally}. Previous SIID research has largely examined external factors such as noise~\citep{sarsenbayeva2018effect,li2025noise}, lighting~\citep{sarsenbayeva2019effect,liu2025understanding}, low temperature~\citep{goncalves2017tapping}, and walking or carrying loads~\citep{li2025estimating}. These factors place constraints on particular perceptual or motor channels. To understand stress-related SIID, we consider two complementary levels of analysis. At the channel level, the questions are which interaction channels are affected and to what extent, depending on the resource demands of the task (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-probes}). At the state level, the questions are how stress unfolds across experimental phases and whether subjective reports, physiological signals, and behavior converge (Sections~\ref{sec:discussion-recovery} and~\ref{sec:discussion-context}). Previous mobile research found that stress affected accuracy and completion time~\citep{sarsenbayeva2019measuring}, whereas our study found selective changes in vocal production and Tower of London timing without a general performance decline. These patterns motivate examining stress in relation to both the interaction being performed and the user's changing state.



\subsection{How the Three Task Probes Responded}
\label{sec:discussion-probes}

We use multiple resource theory (MRT)~\citep{wickens2008multiple} to interpret the differences among the three probes. Each task was dominated by different resources. Target acquisition relied primarily on spatial encoding and manual responses, with low central processing demands. Reading aloud relied primarily on verbal encoding and vocal responses. The Tower of London relied primarily on central executive processing. Taken together, MRT provides a framework for describing the different demands of the three probes.

\paragraph{Target acquisition}

Target acquisition relied primarily on spatial and manual resources and imposed low central demands. No statistically significant differences were detected in any prespecified measure. One possible reason is that direct target selection in VR is a visually guided, closed-loop action. Previous mobile research found that stress affected target acquisition~\citep{sarsenbayeva2019measuring}. This difference may reflect the interaction itself: touchscreen targets impose different precision demands, whereas participants in our study touched targets with a radius of 3~cm in three-dimensional space and received continuous visual feedback. The observed pattern may therefore depend on the target size and feedback available in this task.

\paragraph{Reading aloud}

Reading aloud relied primarily on verbal and vocal resources. After stress induction, mean \(F_0\) increased by 5.55~Hz (approximately 3\%; \(d_z = 0.51\)), while no significant changes were detected in reading duration, speech rate, or active speech ratio. This pattern is consistent with previous research on vocal responses to stress~\citep{kappen2022acoustic,schewski2025measuring,pisanski2018multimodal}. Here, the pitch increase was observed during a constrained reading task after the stressor had ended. Text-constrained speech may therefore retain its overall temporal structure while showing changes in vocal production associated with arousal. The modest size of the pitch shift also illustrates why acoustic measures can reveal changes that are not apparent in coarse measures of task duration.

\paragraph{Tower of London}
The Tower of London requires participants to plan a sequence of moves to reach a goal configuration~\citep{shallice1982specific}. The Gamma models estimated a 36.0\% reduction in execution time and a 24.9\% reduction in total duration from Baseline to Post-Stress. First-move latency, reflecting initial preparation time, and move count, reflecting solution efficiency, showed no significant changes. Participants therefore completed the task more quickly once movement had begun, without a detectable change in the time spent preparing an initial plan or the efficiency of the move sequence.

The interviews offer two possible interpretations of this faster execution. Three participants reported wanting to complete the subsequent tasks more quickly after induction, while seven reported feeling more focused during the post-stress tasks. These accounts suggest that greater urgency or perceived focus may have contributed to a faster pace of task execution. Although participants described changes in how they approached the tasks, the Tower of London measures did not show a corresponding improvement in solution efficiency.

Timing measures in the Tower of London are sensitive to repeated testing~\citep{kostering2015test}. Although execution time rose descriptively after recovery, the recovery comparisons were not significant, and this pattern does not rule out practice effects. The six-move problem with one rollback structure was selected to control structural difficulty across rounds~\citep{kaller2004impact,kaller2012assessing}. At the tested difficulty, faster execution was not accompanied by a significant increase in move count. More demanding problems could help establish whether this timing change persists when planning and monitoring requirements increase.

Across these resource profiles, the observed changes concerned vocal production and Tower of London timing, while no significant differences were detected in the remaining task measures. MRT provides a framework for describing the demands of these probes and generating hypotheses about where changes may occur. Our study did not directly test resource competition, but the task-probe approach helps identify which components of a complex VR workflow warrant closer examination under stress.

\subsection{Stress Induction and Recovery as Distinct Processes}
\label{sec:discussion-recovery}

The induction response and subsequent recovery followed different patterns across measures. During the Induction window, heart rate increased and RMSSD decreased relative to the Baseline window. In the subsequent Post-Stress round, STAI-S and \(F_0\) were elevated, while Tower of London execution time and total duration were shorter than in Baseline.

After the recovery period, STAI-S declined substantially and no longer differed significantly from Baseline, indicating that subjective anxiety had largely subsided. Cardiac measures in the Recovery window also shifted in the direction opposite to induction: heart rate fell below its Baseline-window level, RMSSD returned to a level not significantly different from Baseline, and HF-HRV exceeded Baseline. Cardiac recovery thus involved both movement toward Baseline and shifts beyond Baseline. Participants also removed the headset and rested quietly during Recovery, so the cardiac pattern may partly reflect the change in activity and context.

Behavioural recovery was less clear. Mean \(F_0\) remained close to its Post-Stress value, but neither the Post-Stress--Post-Recovery comparison nor the Baseline--Post-Recovery comparison was significant. Tower of London execution times rose descriptively after recovery, but neither differed significantly from Baseline or Post-Stress. The data therefore do not establish a clear recovery trajectory for these measures. Subjective anxiety may have largely subsided even when the corresponding changes in interaction are less clearly resolved.

These findings illustrate why reactivity and recovery require separate assessment~\citep{linden1997physiological,chida2008chronic}. Effects on cognitive control can also vary with the time elapsed since a stressor~\citep{plessow2011inflexibly}. For VR interaction, the end of the stressor or a reduction in subjective anxiety does not by itself describe the time course of every behavioural measure. Repeated measurements over a longer recovery period would help determine whether vocal and execution changes resolve on similar or different timescales.


\subsection{Subjective Anxiety, Heart Rate, and Task Performance}
\label{sec:discussion-context}

State anxiety reported at the end of the Post-Stress round remained approximately 15 points above Baseline, whereas neither HRV (lnRMSSD and log HF-HRV) nor mean heart rate differed significantly from the Baseline task round. The cardiac response was most evident during induction, while elevated subjective anxiety persisted to the end of the subsequent task round. This divergence is consistent with previous research showing that subjective and physiological stress responses do not always correspond~\citep{campbell2012acute}. It also highlights the importance of distinguishing physiological responses during a stressor from those measured during subsequent interaction.

Higher reported anxiety was also not accompanied by a general decline in task performance. Target acquisition, reading timing, and Tower of London first-move latency and move count showed no significant changes, while \(F_0\) increased and Tower of London execution became faster. Greater subjective distress therefore did not translate into uniformly slower or less efficient task performance. Changes were expressed selectively in vocal production and execution timing, alongside elevated subjective anxiety.

These results illustrate the different information provided by subjective reports, cardiac responses, and task measures. Subjective reports describe how users experience stress, physiological recordings characterise their cardiac responses, and behavioural measures capture changes in specific aspects of interaction. Considering these measures together provides a fuller account of post-stress VR interaction, allowing for differences in their direction and time course.


\subsection{Implications for Stress-Aware VR}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

The application contexts addressed by this study, including teleoperation~\citep{haidegger2011surgery}, simulation-based training~\citep{radianti2020systematic}, social interaction~\citep{li2021social,wei2022communication}, and exposure therapy~\citep{carl2019virtual}, place different demands on users. Our results indicate that support for stress should likewise be differentiated. We did not validate an adaptive system; the following directions are derived from the probe results.

\paragraph{Focus on how a task is performed, not only whether it succeeds}

The findings show the value of examining how users perform a task alongside completion and error measures. Evaluations of VR interaction under stress should include timing and vocal features as well as task outcomes. In social VR and presentation training~\citep{dillon2021real,pertaub2002experiment}, headset audio could provide access to pitch changes even when reading timing shows no significant disruption. Such features indicate further evaluation as part of an assessment that combines vocal, physiological, and self-report measures.

\paragraph{Match support to the task}

At the tested target size, no significant changes were detected in target selection, so the present findings provide no direct basis for a blanket increase in target size under stress. Higher precision demands remain an important case to examine. For planning-heavy tasks such as procedural training~\citep{zhang2026seconds,evangelista2025knowledge}, shorter execution times suggest monitoring changes in pace alongside move efficiency. Subgoal cues, step previews, or verification prompts could be evaluated where rapid execution risks omitted checks or procedural errors. The present task did not show a significant increase in move count, so faster execution alone should not trigger an assumption that the user needs to be slowed down.

\paragraph{Interpret physiological signals in context}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion-context}, elevated subjective anxiety did not coincide with a significant increase in task-round mean heart rate or a general performance decline. Stress-aware systems should therefore interpret cardiac signals in relation to the current activity and consider self-reports and behavioural measures alongside them. The choice of support should be informed by the user's interaction difficulties, current activity, and physiological and behavioural signals.

\paragraph{Continue support after the stressor ends}
The end of a stressor or a reduction in heart rate should not be used alone to determine when support is no longer needed. Subjective anxiety largely subsided after recovery, but the recovery trajectories of \(F_0\) and Tower of London timing were not clearly established. In training debriefs or exposure-based applications, continued assessment of user experience and interaction could help determine whether support remains useful.

Together, these implications support our third contribution: adaptive immersive systems need to consider both whether the user is under stress and what type of interaction the user is performing.


\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion-limitations}

The first limitation is the possibility of learning and order effects. Although task order within each round was counterbalanced using a Latin square, and the reading passages and Tower of London configurations varied across rounds, the phase sequence was necessarily fixed. The effects of practice, fatigue, and familiarization therefore could not be fully separated from those of stress. This issue was considered during pilot testing. Repeating multiple Tower of London and target-acquisition trials within the same session appeared to amplify familiarization, so the main study used a more concise design with one Tower of London problem and one target-acquisition block per round. Residual practice effects nevertheless remain possible. The sample size was comparable to that of many controlled within-participant HCI studies but may still have been insufficient to detect smaller task-specific effects and individual differences.

The Tower of London timing findings were also sensitive to model specification. The contrasts were significant in Gamma mixed-effects models but not in linear mixed models of log-transformed times. The execution-time contrast remained significant when any single participant was excluded, whereas the total-duration contrast did not when either of two participants with long Baseline times was excluded. Execution time therefore showed greater consistency across the participant-exclusion analyses, although both findings require replication. Total duration includes execution time, so the two timing results should not be treated as independent evidence.

Second, the three tasks were intentionally designed as controlled probes of specific interaction components. They allowed us to isolate specific types of interaction but could not fully represent the complexity, task switching, or social and semantic richness of real-world VR use. The findings therefore provide evidence about stress sensitivity in the specific interaction components examined here.

Third, the MRT interpretation has limited scope. Our study did not directly test resource competition in the classical MRT sense: it neither used a dual-task or concurrent-task paradigm nor manipulated competing demands within the same trial. Instead, we compared independent task probes with different dominant demands. MRT therefore provides a framework for task selection and interpretation, with direct tests of resource competition remaining a direction for future research.

Finally, the TSST is unlikely to induce the same ``dose'' of stress in every participant. Previous research indicates that responses to the TSST may depend on ability, engagement, and coping style~\citep{kudielka2007ten}, particularly during mental arithmetic, when participants may differ in response speed, accuracy, and their reactions to errors and interruptions. One advantage of VR is that behavior during induction can be both tightly controlled and continuously recorded. We explored whether performance during mental arithmetic was associated with subsequent changes in the probes but found no robust significant relationship in the present sample. Future research should examine these individual differences systematically in larger samples. All participants were young adults aged 20-31 years recruited from a university, so the results may not generalize to older users or to people with cognitive impairments, elevated baseline anxiety, anxiety-related conditions, or other psychosocial factors. In addition, because Polar recording onset varied, the Baseline window was unavailable for one participant and too short for HF-HRV analysis for another, so some physiological analyses included fewer than 24 participants; baseline-window duration also varied across participants.
